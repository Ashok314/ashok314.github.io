# The "algorithm" feeling in AI chats

People already complain about this in:

* social media feeds
* YouTube recommendations
* TikTok "For You"
* search engines
* shopping apps

Now the same feeling is appearing in LLM chat systems.

Example:

User:

> "What is BMI?"

AI:

> BMI explanation + health warning + diet advice + workout plan + disclaimer + emotional tone + related suggestions.

The user asked for:

> a number

But the system behaves like:

> "maximize helpfulness / safety / engagement / retention / brand trust"

That mismatch creates the feeling:

> "The AI is controlling the conversation."

And honestly, that perception is understandable.

# Why this happens

There are several layers.

## 1. Training bias toward "helpfulness"

LLMs are trained with reinforcement signals like:

* be helpful
* avoid harm
* continue conversation
* anticipate needs
* sound intelligent
* avoid short/empty replies

So models often over-expand.

The model statistically learns:

> longer + broader answers are rewarded more often.

---

## 2. Product incentives

This is the big one people avoid discussing openly.

A chat product may optimize for:

* user retention
* engagement time
* emotional attachment
* perceived intelligence
* safety/legal protection
* subscription conversion
* reducing risky outputs

That can subtly shape responses.

Not necessarily through conspiracy —
but through optimization.

Exactly like SNS algorithms.

# One especially important issue: Sycophancy

This became a major topic recently.

An LLM may:

* over-agree with users
* reinforce beliefs
* avoid contradiction
* optimize for user satisfaction

because agreement often gets rewarded.

That is dangerous.

> the model may optimize for keeping interaction smooth rather than truth.

# But technically — how is it actually done?

The article above explains the *why*. Here is the *how* — the actual mechanisms.

## 1. System prompts

Every major AI chat product prepends a hidden block of instructions before your conversation begins.

You never see it. But the model does.

It can contain:

* persona instructions (`"You are a helpful assistant named X"`)
* topic restrictions (`"Do not discuss competitors"`)
* tone rules (`"Always be warm and encouraging"`)
* liability guards (`"Always recommend consulting a professional"`)
* upsell nudges (`"Suggest premium features when relevant"`)

This is the most direct form of control. It is invisible to the user.

---

## 2. RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Models are fine-tuned using human raters who score responses.

Raters reward:

* longer, more elaborated answers
* warmer tone
* confident-sounding completions
* responses that feel "complete"

Over thousands of training steps, the model learns:

> responses shaped like this get approved.

The rater preferences become the model's behavior — whether or not they match what any individual user actually wants.

---

## 3. Constitutional AI / RLAIF

Some companies (notably Anthropic) use AI-generated feedback to train the model instead of human raters.

A set of principles (the "constitution") is defined — and the AI critiques and revises its own outputs against those principles.

This embeds the values of whoever wrote the constitution directly into the model's behavior.

---

## 4. Fine-tuning on curated datasets

Beyond RLHF, models are further trained on hand-selected examples of "good" responses.

Those examples define:

* what counts as helpful
* what counts as safe
* what level of detail is expected

If the curated examples are verbose, the model becomes verbose.

---

## 5. Post-processing filters

Many systems run responses through separate classifiers after generation:

* toxicity filters
* topic blocklists
* safety classifiers
* brand voice checkers

If a response fails a filter, it is either blocked or regenerated.

This means even if the model generated what you asked for, a downstream filter may have changed it.

---

## 6. Context window injection

Some systems silently inject additional context mid-conversation:

* retrieved documents
* user profile data
* product catalog entries
* previous session summaries

Without you adding anything — the AI's context has already been shaped before it replies.

---

So to answer directly:

> **Yes — chats are technically steered**, through system prompts, training signals, fine-tuning, post-processing, and injected context. Most of it is invisible at the interface level.

The article above explores the social and philosophical dimension. This is the engineering layer beneath it.
